Research by subject

Engineering

en·gi·neer·ing · ingenium: cleverness, ingenuity · ingeniare: to devise, contrive

Engineering is the application of science and mathematics that turns the properties of matter and the sources of energy in nature into things that are useful to people. It's less about asking a question and more about solving a problem — and proving your solution works.

What counts as evidence: A defined spec or requirement, a build or simulation, and measured performance against it — not just “it seems to work.”
  • Write the requirements and success criteria before you build anything.
  • Test under conditions that could make the design fail — not just the easy case.
  • Document every iteration: what you changed and why is often the most interesting part.
  • Simulation (with free tools) is a legitimate substitute for a physical build.
Where it connects

Related areas & concentrations

Engineering is applied science — it turns physics, chemistry, math, and computing into working things. Those neighbors give you the principles your design has to obey.

Related research areas

  • Physics: forces, energy, motion, and materials under load
  • Chemistry & math: how materials behave, and the equations that predict it
  • Computer science: simulation, control, and data from your tests

Sample concentrations

  • Nanoscience & nanotechnology: building and using things at the tiniest scale
  • Engineering photonic materials: designing how materials interact with light
  • Mechanical engineering: forces, machines, and moving parts
  • Applied mathematics for engineers: the modeling behind every design
Where to begin

Four ways into a project

Engineering projects usually take one of these shapes. The build-and-test and simulation routes are the most reachable for a student — and free tools make either one possible.

Design

Design & Build

Make something that meets a requirement — a structure, mechanism, or device — then measure how well it does.

Which truss design holds the most weight for the same amount of material?

Materials

Materials & Nano

Study how a material performs — strength, conductivity, how it handles light or heat.

Does adding fibers to a plaster beam change how much it bends before cracking?

Optimize

Test & Optimize

Take an existing design and make it measurably better on one dimension.

What fin shape gives a model rocket the straightest, highest flight?

Model

Model & Simulate

When you can't build it, model it — with math or free simulation software.

How does wing angle change simulated lift in an airflow model?

The hard part

Turning an idea into a testable project

“Build something cool” isn't a project. Define what success means, change one thing at a time, and measure the result against a spec you set in advance.

1

Define the requirement

What must your design do, and how well? Write the success criteria before you build — that's your finish line.

2

Change one thing at a time

Pick a single design variable to vary — shape, material, angle — and one measure of performance.

3

Build it or simulate it

A physical prototype and a free simulation both count. Make several versions you can compare fairly.

4

Test against the spec — and to failure

Measure performance, then push it until it breaks. Where and how it fails is real data.

Too vague: “Build a better bridge.”
Workable: “Of three truss designs 3D-printed to the same mass, which holds the most weight before it fails?”
Doing the work

Methods & where to look

Pair a way of making with the free tools and literature engineers actually use — you can go a long way without a lab.

Tools & methods

  • CAD & prototyping: FreeCAD, Fusion 360 (free for students), Tinkercad, 3D printing.
  • Simulation: OpenFOAM (fluids/airflow), FEA tools for stress, SPICE for circuits.
  • Electronics: Arduino and breadboards for sensors and control.
  • Testing: measured performance against your success criteria.

Power tools, electricity, and chemicals need adult supervision and safe practice.

Where to find data

  • Papers: IEEE Xplore and arXiv (engineering & applied physics).
  • Materials: MatWeb for material properties; NIST reference data.
  • Journals: ASME and IEEE publications, via Google Scholar.
  • Build ideas: Hackaday and Instructables (for inspiration, not citations).

Cite the standards and papers your design relies on.

The core skill

Prove it against a spec

Anyone can say a design “works.” Engineers show it. Three habits turn a build into evidence.

Specify

Write the requirement and success criteria first. A design isn't finished until it's measured against a target you set in advance.

Test to failure

Don't just confirm the easy case — push the design until it breaks. Knowing where and why it fails is the most useful result.

Iterate

Change one thing, retest, and record it. The log of what you tried and why is the heart of an engineering project.

Build safely: tools, electricity, heat, and chemicals all carry real risk. Work under adult supervision, use protective gear, and follow safe practice — a great result isn't worth an injury.

Report your failures and iterations honestly — they're often more informative than the version that worked.
