Research by subject

Mathematics

math·e·mat·ics · mathēma: that which is learned · manthanein: to learn

Mathematics is the science of numbers and their operations, interrelations, combinations, generalizations, and abstractions — where evidence means proof, and a single counterexample can overturn a claim.

What counts as evidence: A rigorous proof, or a precisely stated and verified computational result.
  • Start from a known theorem and change one condition — what breaks?
  • Compute small cases, conjecture a pattern, then prove it.
  • State definitions precisely before you use them.
  • Expository work counts too: a clear proof of a hard result, written for your peers.
Where it connects

Related areas & concentrations

Mathematics is the language of the sciences. Its neighbors supply real problems — and often inspire new mathematics.

Related research areas

  • Physics: modeling motion, forces, and the laws of nature
  • Engineering: applying math to design and optimize real systems
  • Chemistry: symmetry, rates, and quantitative models of reactions
  • Computer science: algorithms, logic, and computation

Sample concentrations

  • Introduction to combinatorics and graph theory: counting, networks, and connections
  • Coding theory and cryptography: sending messages reliably and securely
  • Fibonacci numbers and visual proofs: patterns you can see and prove
  • Applied mathematics for engineers: the tools that solve real-world problems
Where to begin

Four ways into a question

Most student math research works in one of these areas. All of them start with playing with small examples until a pattern appears.

Combinatorics

Combinatorics & Graphs

Counting arrangements and studying networks of points and connections.

How many ways can you color a map of n regions with three colors so no neighbors match?

Numbers

Number Theory & Patterns

Properties of whole numbers — primes, divisibility, and famous sequences.

Which Fibonacci numbers are divisible by 5, and can you prove the pattern?

Codes

Codes & Cryptography

The math behind correcting errors and keeping information secret.

How many errors can a simple repetition code detect and correct?

Applied

Applied Math & Modeling

Using equations and data to describe and predict real-world systems.

How well does a simple model predict how quickly a cup of coffee cools?

The hard part

Turning an interest into a question

A topic isn't a research question. Narrow to a specific object or problem, explore small cases, and state a precise claim you can try to prove.

1

Start from a pattern or puzzle

A sequence, a game, a shape, a counting problem — something you can explore by hand.

2

Compute small cases

Work out the first several examples, by hand or with code, and record everything.

3

State a precise conjecture

Turn the pattern into an exact claim with clearly defined terms.

4

Prove it — or find out why not

Try induction, contradiction, or a visual argument. A counterexample is a result too.

Too broad: “What's interesting about Fibonacci numbers?”
Workable: “For which n does 3 divide the nth Fibonacci number? Conjecture a rule from small cases and prove it.”
Doing the work

Methods & where to look

Pair the core techniques of proof with the tools and references mathematicians actually use.

Common methods

  • Direct proof: reasoning step by step from definitions.
  • Induction: proving a statement for every whole number.
  • Contradiction: showing the opposite leads to something impossible.
  • Computational exploration: using code to test many cases quickly.
  • Visual proof: arguments built from diagrams and pictures.

Computer checks build confidence, but only a proof settles the question.

Where to find sources

  • Sequences: the On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences (OEIS).
  • References: Wolfram MathWorld for definitions and known results.
  • Preprints: the math section of arXiv for current research.
  • Student journals: Math Horizons and the College Mathematics Journal.

Free tools like Desmos, GeoGebra, and Python are great for exploring patterns.

The core skill

Writing a clear proof

A proof has to convince a careful reader, not just you. Three habits keep your mathematics rigorous and readable.

Define

State every definition and assumption up front. Ambiguous terms are where proofs go wrong.

Justify

Give a reason for every step. “It's obvious” usually hides the hardest part.

Check

Test your claim on edge cases and try to break it. Look for the counterexample before a reader does.

Patterns aren't proofs: a pattern that holds for the first thousand cases can still fail later. Present computation as evidence for a conjecture, and be clear about what you've actually proven.

Cite every theorem you use that you didn't prove yourself, and credit any code or tools.
