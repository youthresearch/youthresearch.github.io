Research by subject

Philosophy

phi·los·o·phy · philos: loving · sophia: wisdom

Philosophy is the study of the nature of existence, knowledge, truth, and ethics. It involves consideration of the most fundamental questions about who we are, and examines philosophical thought across the breadth of history right up to the present day.

What counts as evidence: A clearly stated argument, defended against the strongest objections you can find.
  • State your thesis in one sentence before you start writing.
  • Steelman the opposing view — argue it as well as its best defenders would.
  • Define your key terms explicitly; most philosophical disputes hide in vague definitions.
  • Engage directly with primary texts, not just summaries of them.
Where it connects

Related areas & concentrations

Every field rests on philosophical assumptions, and philosophy draws examples and problems from all of them.

Related research areas

  • Literature: ideas about meaning, morality, and the self, explored through stories
  • History: how ideas emerged, spread, and changed over time
  • Film studies: films that pose philosophical questions, and the philosophy of art
  • Science & technology studies (STS): the ethics and foundations of science and technology

Sample concentrations

  • Descartes' Meditations: doubt, certainty, and the mind–body problem
  • Buddhist culture and philosophy: the self, suffering, and the path to understanding
  • Ethics, religion, and moral thought: where our ideas of right and wrong come from
  • The examined life: what it means to live well, from Socrates to today
Where to begin

Four ways into a question

Most philosophy papers work within one of these branches. All of them need only careful reading, clear thinking, and good arguments.

Ethics

Ethics & Moral Thought

What makes actions right or wrong, and how we should live.

Is it ever wrong to break a promise in order to prevent a small harm?

Knowledge

Knowledge & Certainty

What we can know, how we know it, and what counts as justified belief.

Does Descartes' “I think, therefore I am” actually escape his own doubt?

Mind

Mind & the Self

Consciousness, personal identity, and what makes you the same person over time.

How does the Buddhist idea of “no-self” challenge Descartes' view of the mind?

Society

Politics, Religion & Society

Justice, rights, faith, and the foundations of how we live together.

Can a society be fair if people start with very unequal opportunities?

The hard part

Turning an interest into a question

A big question isn't yet a research question. Narrow to one claim, one or two thinkers, and a thesis you can defend in a single paper.

1

Start from a puzzle that bothers you

A moral dilemma, a claim that seems both true and impossible, an argument you can't quite accept.

2

Narrow to one text or debate

Not “free will” but one thinker's argument, or a specific disagreement between two.

3

Take a position

Turn it into a thesis you can argue for — “X's argument fails because…” or “Y is the better view because…”

4

Test it against objections

What's the strongest reply to your thesis, and can you answer it?

Too broad: “What is the meaning of life?”
Workable: “Does Socrates' claim that ‘the unexamined life is not worth living’ unfairly dismiss people who live well without reflecting on it?”
Doing the work

Methods & where to look

Philosophy's methods are careful reading and careful arguing. Pair them with the texts and references philosophers actually use.

Common methods

  • Close reading: working through a primary text line by line.
  • Argument reconstruction: laying out an argument's premises and conclusion.
  • Conceptual analysis: clarifying exactly what a key term means.
  • Thought experiments: testing ideas against imagined cases.
  • Comparative philosophy: setting thinkers or traditions side by side.

Write out an argument as numbered premises — it shows you exactly where to push back.

Where to find sources

  • Encyclopedias: the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy and the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy.
  • Primary texts: Project Gutenberg for free classic works.
  • Research index: PhilPapers for articles on almost any topic.
  • Journals: Mind and Philosophy & Public Affairs, via Google Scholar or JSTOR.

The Stanford Encyclopedia's bibliographies are a great map of who's argued what.

The core skill

Building an argument

A philosophy paper is only as good as its reasoning. Three habits keep your argument clear and convincing.

Clarity

Define your key terms and state your thesis plainly. If a reader can't restate your claim, it isn't clear yet.

Structure

Make every step follow from the last. Check that your premises are true and actually support your conclusion.

Charity

Present opposing views at their strongest before you respond. Beating a weak version proves nothing.

Argue, don't just report: summarizing what philosophers said is a starting point, not a paper. Your contribution is your own reasoned position and your answers to objections.

Cite every text you discuss, including page or section numbers for quotations.
