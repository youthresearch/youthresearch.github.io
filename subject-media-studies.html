Research by subject

Media Studies

me·di·a stud·ies · medium: middle, go-between · studium: zeal, study

Media studies encompasses the academic investigation of the mass media from perspectives such as sociology, psychology, history, semiotics, and critical discourse analysis.

What counts as evidence: Content analysis, audience data, or platform data, read against a specific claim about effect or meaning.
  • Define your sample precisely — which platform, which time period, which content type.
  • Distinguish what media contains from what effect it has; the second is much harder to show.
  • Coding content for themes needs a clear, consistent rubric applied the same way throughout.
  • Platform-provided analytics are a legitimate data source if you're transparent about their limits.
Where it connects

Related areas & concentrations

Media studies draws on the social sciences and humanities alike. Its neighbors give you theories about audiences, culture, and technology.

Related research areas

  • Culture & gender studies: how media represents identity and shapes culture
  • Psychology: how media affects attention, emotion, and behavior
  • Sociology: how media connects, divides, and organizes communities
  • Education: media literacy and how people learn through media
  • Film studies: how moving images tell stories and create meaning
  • Science & technology studies (STS): how platforms and algorithms shape society

Sample concentrations

  • Media and society: how news, entertainment, and advertising shape public life
  • Mass politics in a media age: campaigns, news coverage, and public opinion
  • Social media and human progress: how online platforms change relationships and activism
Where to begin

Four ways into a question

Most media studies papers work through one of these lenses. All are reachable for a student with public content and careful coding.

Representation

Representation

Who appears in media, how they're portrayed, and what messages that sends.

How do popular films portray characters with mental illness?

News

News & Politics

How news outlets frame events, and how media shapes political opinion.

How did two news outlets frame the same election story differently?

Social

Social Media & Activism

How people use platforms to connect, organize, and build movements.

Is a trending Instagram challenge collective activism or mostly participation?

Audiences

Audiences & Effects

How people choose, interpret, and are influenced by the media they consume.

How do students decide whether a news story they see online is trustworthy?

The hard part

Turning an interest into a question

A topic isn't a research question. Narrow to one platform or outlet, one time period, and something you can count or code consistently.

1

Start from something you've noticed

A trend on social media, a stereotype in TV shows, a headline that seemed slanted.

2

Define your sample

Choose one platform, outlet, or genre, a set time period, and a clear number of items.

3

Make it a comparison

Compare outlets, platforms, or time periods so you have a pattern to explain.

4

Check what you can claim

Are you describing what media contains, or claiming an effect on people? The second needs audience data.

Too broad: “How does social media affect teens?”
Workable: “How do the 50 most-viewed TikTok videos tagged #studytips in one month portray productivity and success?”
Doing the work

Methods & where to look

Pair a method you can carry out consistently with the archives and scholarship media researchers actually use.

Common methods

  • Content analysis: coding media items for themes with a clear rubric.
  • Discourse analysis: reading closely how language frames an issue.
  • Semiotic analysis: unpacking what images and symbols communicate.
  • Audience surveys: asking about media habits, with consent and anonymity.
  • Case study: tracing one campaign, trend, or controversy in depth.

Test your coding rubric on a few items first, then apply it the same way to everything.

Where to find sources

  • Media research: Pew Research Center's reports on news and social media.
  • Archives: the Internet Archive's TV News Archive and the Wayback Machine.
  • News databases: ProQuest or newspaper archives, through a library.
  • Journals: New Media & Society and Journal of Communication, via Google Scholar.

Save screenshots and links with dates — online content can change or disappear.

The core skill

Reading media critically

Media is never just a mirror of reality. Three habits keep your analysis sharp and fair.

Source

Ask who made it, who owns the outlet, and who the intended audience is.

Frame

Notice what's included, what's left out, and how word and image choices steer the viewer.

Effect

Don't assume content changes minds. Claims about effects need evidence about real audiences.

Respect the people behind the posts: when studying social media, focus on public content, avoid naming private individuals, and never share personal information.

Cite every media item, dataset, and study, including the date you accessed online content.
