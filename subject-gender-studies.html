Research by subject

Gender Studies

gen·der stud·ies · genus: kind, type · studium: zeal, study

Gender studies is an interdisciplinary field that examines how gender shapes identity, experience, and representation — and how it interacts with race, class, sexuality, and other parts of identity across society, culture, and institutions.

What counts as evidence: Textual analysis, interviews, surveys, or historical sources, read through a stated theoretical framework.
  • Name the specific framework or scholars you're drawing on early in the project.
  • Ground claims in specific texts, data, or interviews rather than broad generalization.
  • If interviewing people, protect anonymity carefully — this subject can involve sensitive material.
  • Engage with at least one perspective that complicates or challenges your starting position.
Where it connects

Related areas & concentrations

Gender studies is interdisciplinary by design. Its neighbors supply the data, texts, and methods you'll use to make your case.

Related research areas

  • Sociology: how gender shapes families, work, and social institutions
  • Psychology: identity, development, and stereotypes
  • History: how gender roles and rights have changed over time
  • Literature & media studies: how gender is represented in stories and images
  • Political science & international relations: rights, representation, and policy

Sample concentrations

  • Gender equality and global development: education, health, and work around the world
  • Gender and media representation: who appears on screen and in print, and how
  • Gender in education and the workplace: gaps in opportunity, pay, and leadership
Where to begin

Four ways into a question

Most gender studies papers work through one of these lenses. All are reachable for a student through texts, public data, or carefully designed surveys.

Identity

Identity & Experience

How people understand and live their gender, and how it intersects with race, class, and sexuality.

How do students describe the gender expectations they feel at school?

Representation

Representation & Media

How gender is portrayed in books, film, advertising, and social media — and what that teaches audiences.

How are women scientists portrayed in a decade of children's picture books?

Institutions

Work, Law & Policy

How laws, workplaces, and schools create or reduce gender gaps.

How did parental-leave policy changes affect women's employment in one country?

Global

Global Equality & Rights

Gender equality around the world, from girls' education to political representation.

How has the share of women in parliament changed in countries that adopted gender quotas?

The hard part

Turning an interest into a question

A topic isn't a research question. Narrow to a specific group, place, or set of texts, one thing you can examine, and a framework to interpret it.

1

Start from a gap or pattern you noticed

A statistic, a stereotype in media, an unequal rule — something you can point to, not just a feeling.

2

Narrow to a specific context

Not “gender inequality” but one country, one industry, one set of texts, or one time period.

3

Make it a “how / why” question

Ask how a pattern shows up or why it persists, so you have something to explain, not just describe.

4

Check the evidence and the ethics

Can you find texts or data on this, and can you study it without putting anyone's privacy at risk?

Too broad: “Why are there gender stereotypes?”
Workable: “How do toy advertisements from 2000 and 2020 differ in the activities they show boys and girls doing?”
Doing the work

Methods & where to look

Pair a method you can carry out responsibly with the data and scholarship gender researchers actually use.

Common methods

  • Content analysis: counting and coding how gender appears in media or texts.
  • Discourse analysis: reading closely how language frames gender.
  • Surveys: asking about attitudes or experiences, with consent and anonymity.
  • Secondary data analysis: working with existing gender statistics.
  • Historical analysis: tracing how gender roles and rights changed over time.

Interviews on personal topics need consent, anonymity, and adult oversight.

Where to find data

  • Global data: UN Women's data hub and the World Bank's Gender Data Portal.
  • Rankings: the World Economic Forum's Global Gender Gap Report.
  • Politics: the Inter-Parliamentary Union's data on women in parliament.
  • Journals: Gender & Society and Signs, via Google Scholar or JSTOR.

The UN's Sustainable Development Goal 5 reports track gender equality country by country.

The core skill

Reading through a gender lens

Strong gender analysis goes beyond pointing out difference. Three habits keep your work careful and convincing.

Framework

Name the theory you're using — and the scholars behind it — so readers know how you're interpreting evidence.

Intersection

Ask how gender combines with race, class, age, or sexuality. Experiences differ within groups, not just between them.

Evidence

Back every claim with specific texts, data, or responses — and engage the evidence that complicates your view.

This is a sensitive field: topics can be personal for participants and readers. Use respectful, accurate language, protect anyone you study, and work under a teacher's or mentor's guidance.

Cite every source and dataset, and be clear about whose experiences your evidence does — and doesn't — represent.
