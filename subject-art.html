Research by subject

Art (studio & practice-based)

art · ars: skill, craft · praxis: doing, practice

Studio or practice-based research makes art the method: you make work to investigate a question, then study what the making revealed. The finished piece and the thinking behind it are both part of the result.

What counts as evidence: The finished work itself, plus a written account of the question and choices behind it.
  • Decide the question a piece is trying to answer before you start making it.
  • Keep a process journal — the decisions matter as much as the result.
  • Show the work to people outside your circle and record their reactions.
  • A practice-based project still needs a written component explaining the thinking.
Where it connects

Related areas & concentrations

Studio practice overlaps with the humanities and the sciences of perception. Those neighbors are good places to find a second source or a mentor.

Related research areas

  • Humanities: Art History, Design, Media Studies
  • Craft & medium: Illustration, Sculpture, Printmaking, Ceramics, Photography
  • Cross-disciplinary: Psychology of perception, Education, Cultural Studies

Sample concentrations

  • The Self-Portrait: identity and observation through the artist's own face
  • Color & Emotion: how palette and light shape a viewer's feeling
  • Material & Process: what a chosen medium makes possible — or refuses
  • Art & Audience: how a work changes with where and how it's shown
Where to begin

Four ways into a question

A practice-based project reads its own work through one of these lenses. Picking yours tells you what to make and what to pay attention to.

Process

Process & Material

How the medium and the act of making shape the outcome — what happens when you change a tool, surface, or method.

How does switching from digital to physical paint change the marks you make?

Concept

Concept & Meaning

The idea a piece investigates. Art as inquiry — using making to think through a question you can't just write about.

Can a series of drawings say something about memory that words can't?

Form

Form & Composition

How visual choices — color, line, balance, scale — create a specific effect on the viewer.

How does where you place the horizon change the mood of a landscape?

Audience

Audience & Context

How viewers actually respond, and how the setting a work appears in changes what it means.

Does the same piece read differently on a wall versus on a phone screen?

The hard part

Turning an interest into a question

A practice-based project needs a question the making can answer — something you can test by trying it, not just by reading.

1

Start from a making problem you keep hitting

The best questions come from something you struggle with or wonder about in your own work — a color, a subject, a technique.

2

Narrow to one variable you can change

Not “how do I paint better” but one thing: the light source, the medium, the reference, the scale.

3

Frame it as “what happens if…”

Turn it into an experiment you can run by making a small series and comparing the results.

4

Check that you can make and document it

Do you have the materials, the time, and a way to record each stage?

Too broad: “How do I make good art?”
Workable: “How does drawing the same portrait from life versus from a photo change the expression I capture?”
Doing the work

Methods & where to look

In practice-based research the studio is your lab. Pair disciplined making with sources that show how other artists solved the same problem.

Common methods

  • Studio experimentation: make iterations, changing one variable at a time.
  • Process journal: photograph each stage and note the decisions behind it.
  • Reflective writing: an artist statement analyzing what the making revealed.
  • Critique: structured feedback from viewers, recorded and compared.
  • Precedent studies: how other artists handled your problem.

The documentation is the evidence — a beautiful piece with no record of the process is hard to research from.

Where to find sources

  • Visual & free: Google Arts & Culture, Smarthistory, and museum collection sites.
  • Databases: JSTOR and the Art Journal (College Art Association).
  • On method: read about practice-based (or practice-led) research — a recognized way of making art count as inquiry.
  • Technique: artist monographs and craft/technique books for your medium.

Study finished artworks closely, but let your own making and documentation carry the argument.

The core skill

Making that counts as research

What turns a nice piece into research is the loop around it: a clear question, documented making, and honest reflection. Run all three.

Ask

Name the question before you begin, and write down what you expect to happen. This is your hypothesis.

Make & document

Produce a small series, varying one thing. Photograph every stage and journal the choices as you go.

Reflect

Compare the results against what you expected. What did the making reveal that you couldn't have predicted?

Write it up: a practice-based project is only finished when the work is paired with a short written account of the question, the process, and what you learned.

If you work from references or others' images, credit them — and keep your final piece your own.
