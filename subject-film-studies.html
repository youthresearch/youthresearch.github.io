Research by subject

Film Studies

film stud·ies · filmen: membrane, thin skin · studium: zeal, study

Film studies is an academic discipline that deals with various theoretical, historical, and critical approaches to films. It is less concerned with advancing proficiency in film production than with exploring the narrative, artistic, cultural, economic, and political implications of the cinema.

What counts as evidence: Close analysis of specific scenes and technique, supported by production context and scholarship.
  • Anchor every claim to specific timestamps, shots, or edits, not general impressions.
  • Learn basic technical vocabulary (mise-en-scène, editing, sound design) before analyzing with it.
  • Read reviews and scholarship from when the film was released, not just recent takes.
  • A comparison between two films needs a reason — what does pairing them reveal?
Where it connects

Related areas & concentrations

Film sits where art, media, and society meet. Those neighbors give you the theories and context that turn a viewing into an argument.

Related research areas

  • Media studies: how media are made, circulated, and received
  • Art history: visual style, composition, and artistic movements
  • Anthropology: film as a record and expression of culture
  • Sociology: how films reflect and shape social life

Sample concentration

  • Film noir and its contexts: the genre's style, its era, and its modern heirs
Where to begin

Four ways into a question

Most film studies papers work through one of these lenses. All of them start with watching closely — and watching more than once.

Form

Form & Style

How a film creates meaning through cinematography, editing, sound, and mise-en-scène.

How does lighting signal a character's moral ambiguity in one noir film?

Narrative

Narrative & Genre

How stories are structured and how genres set up — and break — audience expectations.

How do modern TV antiheroes borrow from the classic noir protagonist?

History

History & Industry

How films were made, financed, censored, and distributed — and how that shaped what reached the screen.

How did the Production Code change the endings of 1940s crime films?

Culture

Culture & Politics

What films say about identity, power, place, and the moment they were made in.

How do noir films portray the city as a place that limits characters' choices?

The hard part

Turning an interest into a question

A favorite film isn't a research question. Narrow to a small set of films, one technique or theme, and a claim someone could disagree with.

1

Start from something you noticed

A recurring shot, a strange ending, a character type that keeps coming back — note where and when it happens.

2

Narrow to a few films and one element

Not “film noir” but two or three films and one technique, theme, or kind of scene.

3

Make it a “how / why” question

Ask how a technique creates an effect, or why a pattern appears in a particular time and place.

4

Check it's arguable and answerable

Could someone reasonably disagree, and can you support your answer with specific scenes and sources?

Too broad: “Why is film noir so dark?”
Workable: “How do two 1940s noir films use urban settings to limit their protagonists' sense of agency?”
Doing the work

Methods & where to look

Pair a close-reading method with the archives and scholarship film scholars actually use.

Common methods

  • Close reading: shot-by-shot analysis of a key scene.
  • Comparative analysis: setting two or more films side by side.
  • Genre analysis: tracing conventions and how they change over time.
  • Historical & reception study: production records, reviews, and audience response.
  • Theoretical reading: applying a framework such as auteur, feminist, or psychoanalytic theory.

Keep a viewing log with timestamps — it makes citing scenes much easier later.

Where to find sources

  • Databases: the AFI Catalog of Feature Films and the BFI's collections.
  • Archives: the Media History Digital Library for old trade papers and fan magazines.
  • Criticism: Sight and Sound and the Criterion Collection's essays.
  • Journals: Film Quarterly and Cinema Journal, via Google Scholar or JSTOR.

Many classic films are available through your library or free streaming services like Kanopy.

The core skill

Reading a scene closely

Strong film analysis moves from what you see to what it means. Three habits keep your reading specific and convincing.

Describe

Note exactly what's on screen and on the soundtrack — framing, lighting, cuts, music — before interpreting anything.

Interpret

Ask what those choices do: how they shape mood, guide attention, or tell you how to feel about a character.

Contextualize

Connect the scene to the film's genre, its era, and how it was made and received.

Watch with a purpose: see the film once for the experience, then rewatch key scenes with notes. Your argument is only as strong as the specific moments you can point to.

Cite films by title, director, and year, and cite every review and scholarly source you draw on.
