Research by subject

Music

mu·sic · mousikē: art of the Muses · mousa: Muse

Music theory is the study of the practices and possibilities of music. It enables us to understand the structure and meaning behind a musical composition.

What counts as evidence: Score analysis, primary historical sources, or listener/perception data, depending on your approach.
  • Analytical: pick a small corpus (10–20 pieces) and code one feature — harmonic rhythm, form, text setting.
  • Historical: work from primary sources — manuscripts, letters, concert programs, early reviews.
  • Empirical: measure something listeners do — tempo preference, emotion ratings, memory for melody.
  • Cite the edition and recording you used, not just the composer.
Where it connects

Related areas & concentrations

Music is both an art and a cultural practice. Its neighbors help you explain what music means and how people experience it.

Related research areas

  • Media studies: how music is recorded, distributed, and consumed
  • Culture studies: how music expresses and shapes identity and community

Sample concentration

  • Analysis of musical rhythm: meter, pattern, and how listeners perceive and perform rhythm
Where to begin

Four ways into a question

Most music research works through one of these approaches. All are reachable with scores, recordings, and willing listeners.

Theory

Theory & Analysis

How harmony, melody, rhythm, and form are put together in a piece.

How do chord progressions in the chorus differ from the verse across 15 pop songs?

Rhythm

Rhythm & Perception

How listeners hear, feel, and reproduce rhythm, meter, and tempo.

Do listeners tap a five-beat pattern less accurately than a seven-beat pattern?

History

History & Performance

How music was composed, performed, and received in its own time.

How did early reviews describe the premiere of a famous symphony?

Culture

Music & Culture

How music carries identity, tradition, and meaning across communities and media.

How has the use of one traditional instrument changed in modern recordings?

The hard part

Turning an interest into a question

A favorite genre isn't a research question. Narrow to a small set of pieces, one musical feature, and evidence you can actually gather.

1

Start from something you hear

An unusual rhythm, a chord that keeps returning, a style that sounds different from its neighbors.

2

Narrow to a corpus and one feature

Not “jazz” but 10–20 pieces and one element — rhythm, harmony, form, or text setting.

3

Choose your approach

Analyze scores, work from historical sources, or measure how listeners respond.

4

Make it a comparison you can test

Compare pieces, periods, or listener groups so you have a clear pattern to explain.

Too broad: “Why is some music catchy?”
Workable: “Can listeners clap along to a repeating five-beat rhythm as accurately as to a seven-beat one after one minute of listening?”
Doing the work

Methods & where to look

Pair a method that fits your approach with the scores, tools, and scholarship musicians and music researchers actually use.

Common methods

  • Score analysis: reading harmony, melody, rhythm, and form on the page.
  • Corpus study: coding one feature across many pieces.
  • Listening experiments: measuring tapping, ratings, or recall, with consent.
  • Historical research: working from manuscripts, letters, and reviews.
  • Audio analysis: using software to measure tempo, pitch, or timing.

Pick one approach and do it well — mixing too many at once weakens your argument.

Where to find sources

  • Scores: IMSLP, the free Petrucci Music Library of public-domain scores.
  • Notation & audio tools: MuseScore and Sonic Visualiser.
  • Reference: Grove Music Online, through a library.
  • Journals: Music Theory Online and Music Perception, via Google Scholar or JSTOR.

Music Theory Online is free to read and a good model of how theory papers are written.

The core skill

Analyzing what you hear

Good music research connects what's on the page or recording to what it means. Three habits keep your analysis precise.

Listen

Hear each piece several times and mark exact measures or timestamps for the moments you discuss.

Name

Use precise terms — meter, cadence, motif — and define any that your reader might not know.

Explain

Go beyond labeling. Ask what a musical choice does for the listener and why it matters.

Show your evidence: include short score excerpts or timestamps so readers can find exactly what you're describing. If you test listeners, get consent and keep their data anonymous.

Cite the specific edition and recording you used for every piece.
