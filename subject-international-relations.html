Research by subject

International Relations

in·ter·na·tion·al re·la·tions · inter: between · natio: birth, people

International relations is the study of the relations of states with each other and with international organizations and certain subnational entities.

What counts as evidence: Policy documents, treaties, government and NGO data, and comparative case analysis.
  • Pick a specific relationship, treaty, or conflict rather than ‘foreign policy’ broadly.
  • Primary documents (treaties, UN records, government statements) are more useful than news summaries.
  • State your theoretical lens (realism, liberalism, constructivism, etc.) explicitly if you're using one.
  • Compare at least two cases if you're making a general claim about how states behave.
Where it connects

Related areas & concentrations

International relations borrows from across the social sciences. Those neighbors give you theories, data, and the history behind today's headlines.

Related research areas

  • Economics & business: trade, sanctions, aid, and global markets
  • Political science: how governments and institutions make decisions
  • History: the wars, treaties, and empires that shaped today's world
  • Gender studies: how gender shapes security, conflict, and diplomacy
  • Criminology: transnational crime, trafficking, and international law

Sample concentrations

  • Globalization and international migration: how people, goods, and ideas move across borders
  • European integration in comparative perspective: the EU and other regional unions
  • Political risk and prediction: forecasting instability within states
  • Global risk and prediction: anticipating crises that cross borders
Where to begin

Four ways into a question

Most international relations papers work through one of these lenses. All are reachable for a student through public documents and open datasets.

Security

Conflict & Security

Why wars start, how they end, and how states try to keep themselves safe.

How did a ceasefire agreement change the frequency of border clashes?

Institutions

Cooperation & Institutions

How the UN, the EU, and treaties get states to work together — and when they don't.

Why do some countries comply with a treaty while others don't?

Economy

Global Political Economy

Trade, sanctions, aid, and development — where money and power meet.

How did sanctions affect one country's trade partners over five years?

People

Migration & Human Rights

How people move across borders and how states protect — or fail to protect — them.

How do two neighboring countries' asylum policies compare?

The hard part

Turning an interest into a question

A topic isn't a research question. Narrow to specific countries, a specific period, and evidence you can actually find in public records.

1

Start from an event or relationship

A treaty, a conflict, a trade dispute, a migration wave — something with a clear start and real documents behind it.

2

Narrow to specific actors and a time frame

Not “global trade” but two countries, one agreement, and a set number of years.

3

Make it a “how / why” or comparison

Compare two cases or two periods so you have something to explain, not just describe.

4

Check the sources and the lens

Can you find primary documents and data, and which theory will you use to make sense of them?

Too broad: “Why do countries go to war?”
Workable: “Why did two EU member states respond differently to the 2015 refugee crisis, and what does that reveal about European integration?”
Doing the work

Methods & where to look

Pair a method you can carry out with the documents and datasets international relations scholars actually use.

Common methods

  • Comparative case study: setting two or more countries or events side by side.
  • Document analysis: reading treaties, speeches, and UN records closely.
  • Quantitative analysis: working with conflict, trade, or migration datasets.
  • Process tracing: following the chain of decisions behind an outcome.
  • Forecasting: using indicators to assess political or global risk.

Name your theoretical lens early — it tells readers which evidence matters and why.

Where to find data

  • Official records: the UN Digital Library and the UN Treaty Collection.
  • Datasets: the World Bank's open data and the Uppsala Conflict Data Program.
  • Migration: UNHCR's refugee statistics and the IOM's Migration Data Portal.
  • Journals: International Organization and Foreign Affairs, via Google Scholar or JSTOR.

Government foreign-ministry websites publish speeches and statements you can cite directly.

The core skill

Reading sources across borders

Every country tells its own version of events. Three habits keep your analysis balanced and convincing.

Perspective

Ask whose voice a source represents. A government statement, an NGO report, and a news story frame the same event differently.

Comparison

Read sources from more than one country — and more than one side — before you draw a conclusion.

Theory

Use a lens like realism, liberalism, or constructivism to explain why actors behaved as they did, not just what happened.

Be fair to every side: international topics often involve conflict and strong opinions. Represent each country's position accurately, even the ones you disagree with.

Cite every treaty, dataset, and study, and note the date — international situations change quickly.
