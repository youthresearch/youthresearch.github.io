Research by subject

History

his·to·ry · historia: inquiry, knowledge by investigation · histōr: wise one, judge

History is the study of human experience of all kinds, in relation to particular times and places — built from primary sources, read critically, and turned into an argument about the past.

What counts as evidence: Primary sources, read critically for who made them, for whom, and what they leave out.
  • Find primary material you can actually reach: local archives, newspapers, census records, oral histories, digitized collections.
  • Ask who made the source, for whom, and what they left out.
  • Build an argument, not a timeline — a paper that only narrates isn't research.
  • Chicago footnotes are the norm.
Where it connects

Related areas & concentrations

Almost every subject has a history, and history borrows tools from many of them. Those neighbors help you ask sharper questions of your sources.

Related research areas

  • Anthropology & sociology: how communities, cultures, and social structures work
  • Political science: states, power, and how institutions change
  • Philosophy: ideas, beliefs, and how people have reasoned about the world
  • Science & technology studies (STS): how science and technology shape societies
  • Culture studies & art history: what art, objects, and everyday culture reveal

Sample concentrations

  • The Scientific Revolution in early modern Europe: how new ideas about nature took hold
  • Topics in the history and sociology of public health: disease, medicine, and society over time
  • Qing China and the West: conflict and cultural exchange between empires
Where to begin

Four ways into a question

Most history papers work through one of these lenses. All are reachable for a student through digitized archives and local collections.

Politics

Political & Diplomatic

Governments, wars, treaties, and the decisions of people in power.

How did newspapers in two countries report the same treaty differently?

Society

Social History

The lives of ordinary people — work, family, migration, and community.

How did your town's population change after a local factory opened or closed?

Ideas

Science, Ideas & Culture

How beliefs, knowledge, art, and technology developed and spread.

How did pamphlets spread a new scientific idea in the 1600s?

Exchange

Global & Comparative

Contact between peoples and empires — trade, conflict, and cultural exchange.

How did Qing officials describe European traders in their own records?

The hard part

Turning an interest into a question

A period isn't a research question. Narrow to a specific place, time, and set of sources, and ask something you can argue rather than just narrate.

1

Start from sources you can reach

Digitized newspapers, letters, local archives, or oral histories — your question should grow from what survives.

2

Narrow to a place, period, and group

Not “the Industrial Revolution” but one town, one decade, or one group of people.

3

Make it a “how / why” question

Ask why something changed or how people understood it at the time, so you have something to argue.

4

Check it's arguable and answerable

Could another historian disagree, and do your sources actually let you answer it?

Too broad: “What was the 1918 flu pandemic like?”
Workable: “How did two local newspapers explain the causes of the 1918 flu to their readers, and why did they differ?”
Doing the work

Methods & where to look

Pair a method you can carry out with the archives and scholarship historians actually use.

Common methods

  • Archival research: working directly with letters, records, and documents.
  • Source analysis: reading a document closely for its author, audience, and purpose.
  • Comparative history: setting two places or periods side by side.
  • Oral history: recorded interviews, with consent, about lived experience.
  • Historiography: tracing how historians' interpretations have changed.

Keep careful notes of where every source came from — you'll need it for your footnotes.

Where to find sources

  • Newspapers: the Library of Congress's Chronicling America.
  • Digital archives: the Library of Congress, the Internet Archive, and Europeana.
  • Records: national archives and your local historical society or library.
  • Journals: the American Historical Review and Past & Present, via Google Scholar or JSTOR.

Local archives are often underused — and a great source of original research.

The core skill

Reading primary sources critically

A source is never just a window onto the past. Three habits keep your reading careful and your argument convincing.

Source

Ask who made it, when, for whom, and why. Every document has an author with a point of view.

Context

Place the source in its own time. Judge it by what people knew and believed then, not by today's standards alone.

Corroborate

Compare it with other sources. Where accounts agree or clash tells you a lot about what really happened.

Notice the silences: archives often preserve the voices of the powerful and lose everyone else. Ask whose experiences your sources leave out — and say so in your paper.

Cite every source with Chicago-style footnotes, including where you found it.
