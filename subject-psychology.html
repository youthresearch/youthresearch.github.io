Research by subject

Psychology & social science

psy·chol·o·gy · psychē: breath, soul, mind · logos: study

Psychology is the study of the mind and behavior, according to the American Psychological Association — the study of the mind, how it works, and how it affects behavior.

What counts as evidence: Behavioral or survey data collected under a pre-registered design, or careful synthesis of existing studies.
  • Use a validated scale where one exists rather than writing your own questions.
  • Decide your sample and analysis before collecting — no fishing afterward.
  • Get informed consent, and parental consent for participants under 18.
  • Keep responses anonymous and store them somewhere locked.
Where it connects

Related areas & concentrations

Psychology sits between the life sciences and the social sciences. Its neighbors explain the biology beneath behavior and the society around it.

Related research areas

  • Biology & chemistry: the body, hormones, and drugs that shape how we think and feel
  • Neuroscience: the brain systems behind behavior and experience
  • Sociology: how groups and social settings influence individuals
  • Culture studies: how culture shapes thinking, emotion, and identity
  • History: how ideas about the mind and mental health have changed

Sample concentrations

  • Clinical psychology: understanding and treating mental health conditions
  • Psychology of immigration: adjustment, identity, and well-being in a new country
  • Drug use, abuse, and dependence: why addiction develops and how it's treated
  • Stress and health: how stress affects the mind and body
Where to begin

Four ways into a question

Most psychology papers work through one of these lenses. For a student, surveys, simple experiments, and literature reviews are the most reachable.

Cognition

Cognition & Learning

Memory, attention, and decision-making — how people take in and use information.

Do students remember more words when they study with music or in silence?

Health

Stress, Health & Well-being

How stress, sleep, and habits relate to mental and physical health.

Is self-reported sleep related to perceived stress during exam weeks?

Social

Social & Cultural Psychology

How other people, groups, and cultures shape thoughts and behavior.

How do immigrant students describe balancing two cultures at school and at home?

Clinical

Clinical & Abnormal

Mental health conditions, addiction, and what research says about treatment.

What does research show about how school programs affect teen vaping?

The hard part

Turning an interest into a question

A topic isn't a research question. Narrow to one behavior or feeling, a validated way to measure it, and a group you can study safely.

1

Start from something people do or feel

Procrastination, test anxiety, sleep habits, how people remember things — something observable.

2

Find a way to measure it

Use an established scale or a simple task rather than inventing your own questions.

3

Make it a relationship or comparison

Ask whether two things are related, or whether two groups or conditions differ.

4

Check the ethics

Can you study this with consent, anonymity, and no risk of harm to participants?

Too broad: “What causes stress in teenagers?”
Workable: “Among students at my school, is hours of sleep associated with scores on the Perceived Stress Scale during exam week?”
Doing the work

Methods & where to look

Pair a method you can carry out ethically with the measures and literature psychologists actually use.

Common methods

  • Surveys: using validated scales, with consent and anonymity.
  • Simple experiments: comparing performance on a task under two conditions.
  • Correlational studies: testing whether two measures move together.
  • Interviews: exploring experiences in depth, coded for themes.
  • Literature review: synthesizing what published studies have found.

Never diagnose, give treatment, or ask about sensitive topics without adult oversight.

Where to find sources

  • Literature: PubMed and Google Scholar, plus APA PsycInfo through a library.
  • Open science: the Open Science Framework for shared data and pre-registration.
  • Tools: PsychoPy and PsyToolkit for building simple experiments.
  • Journals: Psychological Science and Developmental Psychology.

Many validated scales are published free in the original research articles.

The core skill

Researching with people

When people are your data, design and ethics come first. Three habits keep your study sound and your participants safe.

Consent

Explain the study, get informed consent — and parental consent for anyone under 18 — and let people stop at any time.

Design

Decide your sample, measures, and analysis before you collect data, and stick to the plan.

Caution

Small samples and self-reports have limits. Correlation isn't causation — say what your data can't show.

This is a sensitive field: questions about mental health, drug use, or stress can affect participants. Avoid topics that could cause distress, share support resources, and work under a teacher's or mentor's guidance.

Cite every scale, study, and dataset, and report your results honestly — including ones that didn't turn out as expected.
