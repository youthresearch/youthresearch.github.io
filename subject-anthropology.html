Research by subject

Anthropology

an·thro·pol·o·gy · anthropos: human · logos: study

Anthropology is the scientific and humanistic study of humans — their societies, cultural traditions, and physical development. It traditionally splits into cultural, linguistic, archaeological, and biological anthropology.

What counts as evidence: Fieldwork, interviews, and material culture, read against existing ethnographies.
  • Start with a community, practice, or object you already have some access to.
  • Read at least two ethnographies of similar settings before designing your own study.
  • Be explicit about your own position relative to who you're studying.
  • Get informed consent in writing, and let people opt out of anything specific.
Where it connects

Related areas & concentrations

Anthropology overlaps with the sciences, social sciences, and humanities. These are good places to look for a second source or a co-advisor.

Related research areas

  • Social science: Human Geography, Political Science, Education
  • Science: Evolutionary Biology, Human Genetics, Global Health
  • Humanities: Archaeology, Folklore & Oral History, Art History

Sample concentrations

  • Medical Anthropology: how illness, care, and the body are understood differently across communities
  • Migration & Diaspora Studies: generational identity, remittances, hometown associations
  • Material Culture: what objects mean — clothing, heirlooms, collections
Where to begin

The four subfields

Almost every paper lives mostly inside one of these branches. Choosing yours tells you what evidence you'll gather and what your question can ask.

Cultural

Cultural Anthropology

Living communities — beliefs, rituals, kinship, food, and everyday practice — usually through firsthand fieldwork.

How do second-generation teens decide which holidays “count” as their own?

Linguistic

Linguistic Anthropology

How language shapes identity and power — slang, code-switching, naming, and speaking differently in different settings.

When and why do bilingual students switch languages mid-conversation?

Archaeological

Archaeology

Reconstructs past human life from what people left behind — tools, buildings, trash, and food remains.

What can a local historical collection reveal about how a town lived a century ago?

Biological

Biological Anthropology

Humans as a biological species — evolution, genetics, primates, and how bodies adapt to environments.

How do textbook accounts of human evolution compare with current consensus?

The hard part

Turning an interest into a question

A topic isn't a research question. Narrow along four dials — who, where, when, and what exactly you're comparing.

1

Start from something you actually notice

Curiosity beats importance. The best questions come from something you've seen in your family, school, town, or online.

2

Add a specific group and place

Anthropology is rarely about “people” in general. Anchor it to a community you can reach or a record you can read.

3

Make it a comparison or a “how / why”

Questions that compare two things, or ask how or why, give you something to argue instead of just describe.

4

Check that you can gather evidence

Can you observe, interview, or read something real about this in the time you have?

Too broad: “Why is food important to culture?”
Workable: “How do three families in my community decide which recipes to pass down, and which they let go?”
Doing the work

Methods & where to look

Pair a method you can actually carry out with scholarly sources that model how it's done well.

Common methods

  • Participant observation: joining an activity and taking careful field notes.
  • Ethnographic interviews: open-ended talks in people's own words.
  • Surveys: structured questions for patterns across a group.
  • Archival & material analysis: documents, photos, and objects as evidence.
  • Comparative method: two cases side by side to see what differs and why.

For a school paper, interviews, small-scale observation, and archival work are the most doable.

Where to find sources

  • Databases: Google Scholar, JSTOR, and eHRAF World Cultures (Yale's ethnographic archive).
  • AnthroSource: the American Anthropological Association's journals.
  • Key journals: American Anthropologist, Current Anthropology, Cultural Anthropology, and the Annual Review of Anthropology for overviews.
  • Your library: a librarian can get you paywalled articles free.

Scholarly ≠ popular — build your argument on peer-reviewed work, not blogs or news.

Non-negotiable

Research ethics

Anthropology studies real people, so ethics is the core of doing the field responsibly — not optional paperwork.

Before you talk to anyone: settle consent, privacy, and adult oversight first.
  • Informed consent: explain who you are and how you'll use what people say; let them stop anytime.
  • Protect privacy: use pseudonyms and leave out identifying details.
  • Ask an adult first: loop in a teacher or mentor, and check whether your school needs approval to interview minors or vulnerable people.
  • Cultural humility: describe communities on their own terms; watch for ethnocentrism.
  • Name your positionality: be honest about how your background shapes what you notice.
