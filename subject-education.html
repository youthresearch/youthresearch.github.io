Research by subject

Education

ed·u·ca·tion · educare: to bring up, train · educere: to lead out

Education research studies education and learning themselves — the people, interactions, organizations, and institutions that shape how, and how well, we learn. It's the science of what actually helps people learn, and how you'd know.

What counts as evidence: Classroom data, surveys with validated instruments, or controlled comparisons of a method, tool, or intervention — not just opinion about what “should” work.
  • Studying your own school needs your teacher's or administration's sign-off first.
  • Use an existing, validated survey instrument rather than writing your own from scratch.
  • Compare a change against a real baseline or control group — not just before-and-after.
  • Small samples are common here — be honest about what they can and can't show.
Where it connects

Related areas & concentrations

Education borrows from every social science — because learning is shaped by minds, cultures, histories, and institutions all at once. Those neighbors give you theories and methods.

Related research areas

  • Psychology: how people learn, remember, and stay motivated
  • Sociology & anthropology: how communities and cultures shape schooling
  • History & political science: how school systems and policy came to be

Sample concentrations

  • Foundations of education: the big ideas underneath how we teach
  • Foundations of American education: one system's history and structure
  • On education — fundamentals, philosophies, realities: theory meets the classroom
Where to begin

Four ways into a question

Most education projects work through one of these lenses. The survey- and data-based ones are the most reachable for a student — often right inside your own school.

Learning

How People Learn

Memory, motivation, attention, study habits — the psychology of learning itself.

Does spacing out study sessions relate to better quiz scores than cramming?

Teaching

Methods & Tools

What actually works in the classroom — teaching styles, activities, and edtech.

Does a flashcard app beat paper flashcards for vocabulary retention?

Equity

Access & Equity

Who gets what kind of education — and the gaps between groups.

What shapes whether girls at your school pursue or drop STEM classes?

Systems

Systems & Policy

Schools, curricula, and institutions — how they're built and how they change.

How do two schools' schedules relate to reported student stress?

The hard part

Turning an interest into a question

A topic isn't a research question. Narrow to one group, one thing you can measure, and a fair comparison — and get permission before you study anyone.

1

Get permission first

Studying classmates or your school means clearing it with a teacher or administrator, and keeping responses anonymous.

2

Narrow to one group and one outcome

Not “does tech help learning” but one class, one tool, one measurable result — like quiz scores.

3

Build in a fair comparison

Compare against a baseline or a control group, so a change means something — not just before-and-after.

4

Measure it honestly

Use a validated survey or a clear score, and watch for what else could explain the result.

Too broad: “Does technology help students learn?”
Workable: “In two of my school's classes, do students who use flashcard app X for two weeks score higher on a vocab quiz than students using paper flashcards?”
Doing the work

Methods & where to look

Pair a method you can run safely with the databases and datasets education researchers actually use.

Common methods

  • Surveys: attitudes, habits, and experiences — using validated questions.
  • Classroom comparisons: a method or tool tested against a control.
  • Observation: watching how a class actually works.
  • Interviews: the “why” behind students' choices.
  • Open-data analysis: working with existing education datasets.

Anything involving students needs consent, anonymity, and adult sign-off.

Where to find data

  • The database: ERIC — the Education Resources Information Center.
  • Statistics: NCES (US), OECD's PISA, and the World Bank's EdStats.
  • Journals: the American Educational Research Journal and Review of Educational Research.
  • General: Google Scholar and JSTOR for the wider literature.

Your own teachers and school counselors can be great first sources.

The core skill

Studying learning fairly

Learning is influenced by dozens of things at once, so education claims are easy to get wrong. Three habits keep yours honest.

Compare

Measure against a real baseline or control group — a “before and after” with nothing to compare to proves little.

Measure well

Define what “learning” means for your study, and use a validated instrument rather than a question you invented.

Mind the confounds

Motivation, prior ability, and the teacher all affect results — account for what else could explain your finding.

Ethics with people: studying classmates or minors means informed consent, anonymous responses, and a teacher's or administrator's approval — keep all data private and work under a mentor's guidance.

Cite every study and dataset, and be clear about what a small, single-school sample can — and can't — show.
