Research by subject

Sociology

so·ci·ol·o·gy · socius: companion, ally · logos: study

Sociology is the systematic study of human social institutions and social relationships — explaining patterns in how groups, not just individuals, live, organize, and change.

What counts as evidence: Survey data, interviews, or public social datasets, analyzed for patterns across groups.
  • Census and public survey data (GSS, ACS) support real analysis with zero data collection needed.
  • Be explicit about which group or population your claim is actually about.
  • Interview-based work needs a consistent question guide and careful thematic coding.
  • Watch for confounds — apparent group differences often trace back to other variables.
Where it connects

Related areas & concentrations

Sociology shares questions with almost every social science. Its neighbors give you theories, methods, and data to explain how groups behave.

Related research areas

  • Anthropology: culture, community, and everyday life
  • History: how societies and institutions changed over time
  • Political science: power, participation, and public policy
  • Culture & gender studies: identity, media, and representation
  • Criminology: crime, deviance, and how societies respond
  • Psychology: how individuals think and act within groups

Sample concentrations

  • Refugees, migrants, and the stateless: displacement, belonging, and rights
  • Hyphenated Americans: identity and community across cultures
  • Gender and political conflict: how gender shapes power and conflict
  • Social media and human progress: how online life changes relationships and society
Where to begin

Four ways into a question

Most sociology papers work through one of these lenses. All are reachable for a student through public data, surveys, or interviews.

Inequality

Inequality & Stratification

How income, education, race, and gender shape people's opportunities.

How does college attainment differ across neighborhoods in your county?

Identity

Identity, Migration & Community

How people build belonging across cultures, places, and generations.

How do second-generation immigrant students describe their sense of identity?

Institutions

Institutions & Social Life

Families, schools, religion, and work — the structures that organize daily life.

How have family sizes changed in your state over the past fifty years?

Change

Technology & Social Change

How new technologies, movements, and media reshape relationships and society.

Does time on social media relate to how connected students feel to their friends?

The hard part

Turning an interest into a question

A topic isn't a research question. Narrow to a specific group and setting, one outcome you can measure or describe, and evidence you can actually collect.

1

Start from a pattern you've noticed

Who sits together at lunch, who uses which apps, which neighborhoods have which resources.

2

Narrow to a group and setting

Not “teenagers” but students at one school, one age range, or one community.

3

Make it a “how / why” or comparison

Compare groups or explain a pattern, so you have something to analyze, not just describe.

4

Check the data and the ethics

Can you use public data, or survey people with consent and anonymity?

Too broad: “How does social media affect society?”
Workable: “Among students at my school, is daily time on social media associated with how many close friends they report having in person?”
Doing the work

Methods & where to look

Pair a method you can carry out responsibly with the data and scholarship sociologists actually use.

Common methods

  • Secondary data analysis: working with census or public survey data.
  • Surveys: designing questionnaires, with consent and anonymity.
  • Interviews: in-depth conversations, coded for themes.
  • Observation: watching patterns in public settings.
  • Content analysis: studying media, social posts, or documents.

Surveys and interviews of minors usually need adult oversight and approval.

Where to find data

  • Surveys: the General Social Survey (GSS) and Pew Research Center.
  • Census: the U.S. Census Bureau's American Community Survey (ACS).
  • Archives: ICPSR for thousands of social science datasets.
  • Journals: American Sociological Review and Social Forces, via Google Scholar or JSTOR.

The GSS Data Explorer lets you analyze decades of survey data in your browser.

The core skill

Thinking sociologically

Sociology looks past individual choices to the patterns around them. Three habits keep your analysis sharp and fair.

Patterns

Look for trends across groups rather than single stories. One example isn't a pattern.

Context

Ask how institutions, history, and resources shape what people do — not just personal choice.

Confounds

Check whether another variable — like income or age — explains a difference you see between groups.

Respect your participants: get informed consent, keep responses anonymous, and describe every group fairly. Work under a teacher's or mentor's guidance.

Cite every dataset and study, and be clear about which population your findings describe.
