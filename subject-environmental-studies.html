Research by subject

Environmental Studies

en·vi·ron·men·tal · environ: to surround, encircle · -ment: the result of

Environmental studies is the multidisciplinary field that examines how humans interact with the environment — pulling together science, policy, economics, and society to understand problems that no single subject can solve alone.

What counts as evidence: Environmental data combined with policy, economic, or social analysis — the science and the human side, together.
  • Pick a specific local issue (a watershed, a zoning decision) rather than “climate change” broadly.
  • Public environmental monitoring data (EPA, NOAA, USGS) is free and detailed — start there.
  • Separate the scientific claim from the policy claim — they need different kinds of evidence.
  • Interview stakeholders on more than one side of an issue, not just the side you agree with.
Where it connects

Related areas & concentrations

Environmental studies borrows from the sciences and the social sciences at once — because every environmental issue is part nature, part people. Those neighbors give you both the data and the human context.

Related research areas

  • Ecology, biology & chemistry: the natural systems and what's happening in them
  • Physics & engineering: energy, climate, and the built environment
  • Economics & business: the costs, incentives, and trade-offs behind decisions

Sample concentrations

  • Comparative environmental politics: how different places govern the environment
  • Buildings, energy & the environment: the footprint of how we build and power things
  • Understanding earthquakes & prediction: natural hazards and how we forecast them
  • Water quality & global environmental health: the environment's effect on people
Where to begin

Four ways into a question

Most environmental-studies projects work through one of these lenses. All are reachable with free public data — and the best ones connect the science to a real human decision.

Science

Monitoring & the Physical Environment

Measure the environment itself — water, air, soil, or natural hazards like earthquakes.

How has a local stream's water quality changed over the last decade?

Policy

Politics & Policy

How governments and institutions respond — and why some approaches work better.

How do two cities' recycling policies compare on actual results?

Energy

Energy & the Built Environment

The footprint of how we build, power, and move — and how to shrink it.

Could your school cut energy use by changing one building habit?

People

Environment, Health & Justice

Who bears the costs of environmental harm — and who gets the benefits.

Does air quality differ between neighborhoods in your area?

The hard part

Turning an interest into a question

“The environment” is too big to research. Zoom in on one place, one measurable thing, and keep the science question separate from the policy question.

1

Go local and specific

One stream, one neighborhood, one policy, one building — not “pollution” or “climate change” in general.

2

Split the science from the policy

“Has the water gotten more polluted?” is a science question; “is the current rule working?” is a policy one. Know which you're asking.

3

Find the public data

EPA, NOAA, and USGS publish detailed monitoring data — often for your exact county.

4

Hear more than one side

Talk to (or read) stakeholders who disagree — residents, officials, businesses — not just your own side.

Too broad: “How do we stop climate change?”
Workable: “Using public EPA data, how has air quality near one local highway changed over ten years, and what policies changed in that time?”
Doing the work

Methods & where to look

Pair a method you can run safely with the public datasets and literature environmental researchers actually use.

Common methods

  • Field sampling: water, air, or soil measurements — done safely.
  • Open-data analysis: working with existing monitoring datasets.
  • Mapping (GIS): showing where impacts fall across an area.
  • Policy analysis: studying a law or program and its effects.
  • Surveys & interviews: capturing what stakeholders think.

Field sampling needs safety planning, permission, and care for the site.

Where to find data

  • US agencies: the EPA (air/water), NOAA (climate), and USGS (water, earthquakes).
  • Global: NASA Earthdata and Our World in Data.
  • Journals: Environmental Science & Technology and Global Environmental Change.
  • General: Google Scholar and JSTOR for the wider literature.

Local agencies and watershed groups often share data too.

The core skill

Separate the science from the politics

Environmental questions blend facts and values, and it's easy to blur them. Three habits keep your work fair and credible.

Localize

Anchor your project to a specific place and issue — that's where you can actually gather evidence and say something true.

Separate claims

Keep the scientific finding (“what is happening”) apart from the value judgment (“what we should do”) — they rest on different evidence.

Hear all sides

Seek out the people and data that challenge your view, not just the ones that confirm it.

Tread lightly & fairly: plan for safety in the field, disturb sites and habitats as little as possible, get permission on private or protected land, and represent every community and stakeholder honestly.

Cite every dataset and study, and be clear about what your evidence can — and can't — prove.
