Research by subject

Linguistics

lin·guis·tics · lingua: tongue, language · -ics: the study of

Linguistics is the scientific study of language — how sounds, words, and sentences are structured, how people use language to communicate meaning, and how languages vary and change across communities and over time.

What counts as evidence: Language data — recordings, corpora, or elicited examples — analyzed against a specific claim about structure or use.
  • Open corpora (COCA, and others) let you test claims about real usage patterns for free.
  • Be precise about which level you're studying: sound, structure, meaning, or use.
  • If recording speakers, get consent and anonymize identifying details.
  • A small, well-transcribed dataset beats a large, sloppy one.
Where it connects

Related areas & concentrations

Language touches nearly every part of human life. Linguistics borrows from — and contributes to — fields across the sciences and humanities.

Related research areas

  • Psychology & neuroscience: how the brain learns, stores, and processes language
  • Anthropology & sociology: how language shapes identity, culture, and community
  • Literature: style, meaning, and the craft of written language
  • Computer science: natural language processing and speech technology
  • Philosophy: meaning, reference, and the nature of language itself

Sample concentrations

  • Language acquisition: how children and adults learn first and second languages
  • Sociolinguistics: dialects, slang, and how speech varies across groups
  • Language change and endangered languages: how languages evolve, spread, and disappear
  • Language and technology: how computers understand and generate human language
Where to begin

Four ways into a question

Most linguistics papers work at one of these levels. All are reachable for a student through corpora, recordings, or careful observation.

Sound

Sounds & Speech

Phonetics and phonology — how speech sounds are produced, perceived, and organized.

Do speakers from two regions pronounce the same vowel differently?

Structure

Words & Sentences

Morphology and syntax — how words are built and combined into sentences.

How often do new English words form by blending, like “brunch” or “smog”?

Meaning

Meaning & Use

Semantics and pragmatics — what words mean and how context shapes what we understand.

How has the meaning of “literally” shifted in news writing over the past few decades?

Society

Language & Society

How language varies with region, age, and identity — and how it changes over time.

How do bilingual students decide when to switch between their languages?

The hard part

Turning an interest into a question

A topic isn't a research question. Narrow to one feature of language, one group of speakers or texts, and data you can actually collect.

1

Start from something you've heard or read

A new slang term, a regional accent, a word whose meaning seems to be changing.

2

Pick one feature and one source of data

Not “how teens talk” but one word, sound, or construction in one corpus or group of speakers.

3

Make it a comparison

Compare across time, regions, age groups, or contexts so you have a pattern to explain.

4

Check your data and consent

Is there a corpus you can search, or can you record speakers with their permission?

Too broad: “How is the internet changing language?”
Workable: “How has the use of ‘like’ as a quotation marker (‘she was like, …’) changed in the Corpus of Contemporary American English since 1990?”
Doing the work

Methods & where to look

Pair a method you can carry out with the corpora, tools, and references linguists actually use.

Common methods

  • Corpus analysis: searching large text collections for usage patterns.
  • Acoustic analysis: measuring speech sounds from recordings.
  • Surveys & judgments: asking speakers which sentences sound natural.
  • Interviews & recordings: collecting natural speech, with consent.
  • Comparative analysis: setting two languages or dialects side by side.

Transcribe carefully and consistently — your analysis is only as good as your transcripts.

Where to find data

  • Corpora: the Corpus of Contemporary American English (COCA) and Google Books Ngram Viewer.
  • Sound analysis: Praat, free software for analyzing speech.
  • World languages: the World Atlas of Language Structures (WALS) and Ethnologue.
  • Journals: Language and Journal of Sociolinguistics, via Google Scholar or JSTOR.

The Linguistic Society of America's website has accessible introductions to every subfield.

The core skill

Describing language like a linguist

Linguists study how language is actually used, not how it “should” be. Three habits keep your analysis scientific.

Describe

Record what speakers really say and write. Every dialect follows rules — none is “wrong.”

Precision

Name your level — sound, word, sentence, meaning, or use — and define your terms clearly.

Data

Back every claim with real examples, and count them. One example shows a possibility, not a pattern.

Respect your speakers: get permission before recording anyone, remove names and identifying details, and describe every way of speaking with respect.

Cite every corpus, dataset, and study, and note the dates your data covers.
