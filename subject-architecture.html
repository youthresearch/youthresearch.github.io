Research by subject

Architecture

ar·chi·tec·ture · arkhi: chief · tekton: builder

Architecture is the art and science of designing and engineering large structures and buildings — and studying how those buildings and spaces shape the people who use them.

What counts as evidence: Drawings, site visits, historical plans, and how people actually use a space versus how it was designed.
  • Pick a building or site you can visit repeatedly, not just photograph once.
  • Compare the architect's stated intent against how people actually use the space.
  • Learn to read a floor plan and section before making claims about one.
  • Cite the drawings and sources you used, not just the building's name.
Where it connects

Related areas & concentrations

Architecture sits between the humanities, engineering, and the social sciences. Those neighbors are good places to find a second source or a co-advisor.

Related research areas

  • Humanities: Art History, History, Culture Studies
  • Science & technology: Civil & Structural Engineering, Materials Science, Environmental Science
  • Social science: Urban Studies, Human Geography, Environmental Psychology

Sample concentrations

  • The Skyscraper: how the tall building became possible and what it changed
  • New York City from an architectural perspective: reading one city through its buildings
  • The Medieval Mediterranean: a confluence of cultures in stone and space
  • Sustainable approaches to architectural heritage: keeping old buildings useful and low-impact
Where to begin

Four ways into a question

Most architecture papers look at a building through one of these lenses. Picking yours tells you what evidence to gather and what your question can ask.

Design & theory

Design & Theory

How form, proportion, light, and space are composed — why a building looks and feels the way it does.

What makes one reading room feel calm and another feel tense?

History

History & Heritage

How styles and buildings changed over time, and how we decide what to preserve or adapt.

How has one local building been reused across a century of changing needs?

Structure

Structure & Materials

How buildings actually stand up — the engineering, materials, and constraints behind the form.

How did steel framing make the early skyscraper possible?

Society

Space & Society

How buildings and cities shape behavior, access, and community — urbanism and environmental psychology.

How does a plaza's layout decide where people gather or avoid?

The hard part

Turning an interest into a question

A topic isn't a research question. Narrow to one building, one feature, and one thing you can actually find out about it.

1

Start from a place you can reach

The best questions come from a building or space you can visit more than once, or one you can find real drawings and records for.

2

Narrow to one building and one feature

Not “skyscrapers” but one tower; not “the whole building” but its entrance, its light, or its stairs.

3

Make it a “how / why” or a comparison

Questions that ask how or why, or compare two buildings, give you something to argue instead of just describe.

4

Check that you can gather evidence

Can you visit it, read its plans, or find its history in the time you have?

Too broad: “Why are skyscrapers important?”
Workable: “How does the seating layout of my school library shape where students choose to sit and study?”
Doing the work

Methods & where to look

Pair a method you can actually carry out with sources that show how architects and historians build an argument.

Common methods

  • Site visits & observation: repeat visits, noting light, flow, and how people really use the space.
  • Reading drawings: plan, section, and elevation (see below).
  • Precedent studies: comparing two or three buildings that solve the same problem.
  • Archival research: old plans, permits, and historical photos.
  • Behavioral mapping: sketching where people sit, walk, and gather.

For a school paper, a repeat site visit plus a few precedent studies is very doable.

Where to find sources

  • Databases: the Avery Index to Architectural Periodicals, JSTOR, and Google Scholar.
  • Journals: Journal of the Society of Architectural Historians (JSAH) and Architectural Record.
  • Archives: HABS/HAER at the Library of Congress (measured drawings of historic U.S. buildings), plus your city's planning office and local historical society.
  • Learn to read drawings: Francis D. K. Ching, Architecture: Form, Space, and Order.

Scholarly ≠ popular — sites like ArchDaily are great for examples, but build your argument on peer-reviewed work.

The core skill

Reading a building

Almost every claim in an architecture paper is backed by a drawing. Three views describe any building — learn to read all three before you write about one.

Plan

A view straight down, with one floor sliced horizontally. Shows how rooms connect and how you move through.

Section

A vertical cut through the building. Shows heights, how floors stack, and the spaces you can't see in plan.

Elevation

A straight-on view of one exterior face. Shows proportion, windows, materials, and style.

Then watch it in use: the most interesting findings come from the gap between how a space was drawn and how people actually use it.

On site: get permission before entering or photographing private property, stay safe, and note the date and time of each visit so your observations are citable.
